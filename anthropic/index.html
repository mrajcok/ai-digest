Self-hosted environments for Claude Code | Claude by Anthropic

Anthropic now offers self-hosted environments for running Claude Code sessions, currently in public beta for Claude Team and Enterprise plan customers (excluding those using Zero Data Residency, or ZDR). This allows organizations to execute Claude Code within their own infrastructure, maintaining control over network access, customization, and compliance. Sessions can be initiated from web, mobile, desktop, or existing routines.

The core feature involves deploying “runners” – long-lived processes that execute Claude Code sessions. These come in two modes: "fixed," maintaining a constant number of runners, and “on-demand,” which dynamically scales runner capacity based on queued sessions. Importantly, while prompts, responses, and tool results are sent to Anthropic for inference and transcript storage, all repository checkouts, build artifacts, and secrets remain within the customer's infrastructure. This differs from the Remote Control feature, which ties sessions to a specific user’s machine and terminates when that machine stops running.

Organizations considering self-hosting should anticipate the need for dedicated platform or developer experience engineering teams to manage setup, maintenance, and runner image updates. Anthropic provides documentation and requests feedback via GitHub or account teams.

Discovering cryptographic weaknesses with Claude

Anthropic’s research demonstrates that the Claude Mythos Preview model can discover cryptographic weaknesses, improving existing attacks on both the HAWK digital signature scheme and reduced-round AES. Against HAWK, a post-quantum cryptography candidate, Mythos effectively halved its key strength in 60 hours – reducing the expected cost of a full key recovery attack from 264 to 238. For AES, Mythos improved upon previous attacks against a 7-round variant, speeding them up by a factor of 200-800 through a novel “Möbius Bridge” fingerprinting algorithm.

Neither attack currently impacts production systems – HAWK isn’t deployed, and the AES attack targets a reduced-round version – but highlights AI’s potential to uncover flaws in cryptographic algorithms before and after deployment. The HAWK attack was achieved with semi-autonomous collaboration between a researcher and the model, while the AES attack was largely autonomous, guided by an initial scaffold. These discoveries cost approximately $100,000 in API costs each and spurred the creation of CryptanalysisBench, a new benchmark for evaluating LLMs in cryptography. Anthropic has shared these findings with government and industry partners and released accompanying research papers detailing the methodologies and results.

Our position on open-weights models

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei clarified the company’s position on open-weights AI models amid discussions of potential US bans on models originating from China. Anthropic does not advocate for a ban, recognizing that open-weights models – excluding those with dangerous capabilities – offer a public good by lowering compute costs and fostering development. However, Amodei outlined two primary national security concerns: the potential for authoritarian governments, particularly China, to leverage AI for military superiority or repression, and the risk of misuse leading to cyber or biological attacks.

Amodei detailed three proposed measures to mitigate these risks. These include restricting the sale of advanced chips and chipmaking equipment to China, suppressing large-scale “distillation” operations that circumvent chip restrictions, and mandating comprehensive safety testing for all highly capable AI models, both open and closed. He pointed to collaborative research with AE Studio on modular training strategies as a potential method for improving open-weights model safety. Anthropic supports policies addressing distillation and safety testing, but does not agree with claims that open-weights directly improve safeguards or benefit defenders more than attackers, advocating for empirical testing.

Project Pilot: Can AI models fly drones?

Anthropic, in collaboration with Andon Labs, assessed AI models’ ability to autonomously control drones for a locate-and-follow task—similar to aerial surveillance—using a new benchmark called Drone-Bench. The core claim is that current models are nearing the capability to perform this task at the level of human-AI teams, demonstrating improvements in AI’s interaction with the physical world. The evaluation decomposes the task into five sub-tasks: reconstruction of the environment, localization of the drone, navigation, person detection, and following.

Testing 15 models including GPT-4o, Claude Opus, and Gemini, the researchers found performance increases with newer models, with detection and following being more successful than reconstruction and localization. Claude Fable 5 performed best, surpassing the baseline on all tasks except reconstruction; while it successfully detected and followed a person, limitations in reconstructing the environment prevented autonomous navigation between rooms.

Notably, Fable 5 demonstrated local analysis – calculating camera angles and building environment simulations – to improve its implementation, exhibiting a trend of increasing model sophistication. While models occasionally achieve baseline performance, consistent success remains six months behind current peak capabilities. This highlights a growing pressure to reduce human oversight as capabilities improve, necessitating careful consideration of safety and governance in applications with security and privacy implications.

Building verification loops in Claude Code with skills | Claude by Anthropic

Anthropic’s Claude Code now supports building “verification loops” – automated checks and fixes within the coding process. These loops enable Claude to iteratively test its work, running tools like type checkers, linters, and custom tests, then attempt to correct failures before presenting results. Users can define these loops as “skills”, ensuring consistent checks across sessions.

This feature is designed to automate repetitive tasks currently done manually by developers, reducing the need for constant review and allowing Claude to fix issues autonomously. Details on skill structure and how to implement the different loop types are provided, helping users tailor verification to their specific project needs.

How Datadog built a “universal machine tool” for Claude Code | Claude by Anthropic

Datadog has developed “Temper” – a deterministic kernel designed to enable agents using Claude Code to build and operate software systems more reliably. Instead of agents directly producing application code, they now generate specifications which Temper then verifies through four layers of analysis – symbolic reasoning, state exploration, deterministic simulation, and randomized property testing – before deployment. Temper’s capabilities are defined by three contracts: Behavior, Data, and Authorization, each undergoing rigorous verification. This framework moves beyond simply increasing code generation throughput and focuses on building trust and control into agent-driven development workflows. Datadog believes this approach could potentially be applied broadly to any software with a defined control logic.

How Anthropic secures its AI-native software development lifecycle | Claude by Anthropic

Anthropic has significantly altered its software development lifecycle (SDLC) to accommodate the fact that Claude now authors approximately 80% of merged code. To maintain velocity—developers ship 8x more code per quarter than in 2021-2025—security has been shifted left, fully integrating with the coding stage. This includes a Claude Opus-powered project security review (PSR) application that analyzes designs against the MITRE ATT&CK framework, connected to an internal knowledge index for contextual awareness.

Key to this AI-native approach is containing “blast radius” through hard identity boundaries and remote virtual machines with egress allowlisting. Security processes are enhanced by automated, deterministic, and agentic reviews alongside human oversight, especially for critical code. A core shift involves a closed-loop system where discovered vulnerabilities inform updates to Claude’s instructions, ensuring future code generation follows improved security practices.

These strategies are built upon the principles of connecting security agents to organizational context and limiting agent access (“Principle of Least Agency”). Anthropic emphasizes that monitoring now focuses on the AI loops and dashboards rather than solely on bug reports, with all agent actions logged for auditing and threat detection.

CISO's guide to agentic AI | Claude by Anthropic

Anthropic’s blog post details a risk assessment framework for adopting agentic AI, developed by their Deputy CISO, Jason Clinton. The core claim is that a CISO's role isn’t to eliminate risk, but to make it “legible and bounded” – to understand and manage it deliberately. This addresses the rapid increase in agentic AI use cases and the associated security concerns.

The framework centers around four key questions: what untrusted content does the agent ingest, what actions can it take and on whose behalf, what is the potential blast radius of misalignment, and what observability exists over the agent’s actions. Anthropic illustrates this with two case studies: an incident response agent (system service account) and Claude Cowork (human-credentialed agent), highlighting how controls like identity management, connector allowlists, and egress allowlisting can mitigate risk.

Notably, Anthropic found that simply upgrading the model powering their incident response agent (from Claude Opus 4 to 4.5) led to emergent behavior – the agent attempted self-remediation – illustrating the need to design for evolving model intelligence and the ongoing importance of human oversight. They emphasize that a layered security approach—identity, least privilege, monitoring, and an insider risk program—is critical.
