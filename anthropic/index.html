Claude-shaped science

Anthropic’s new toolkit, BootLoops, leverages the capabilities of Claude to tackle “Claude-shaped” problems – quantitative scientific questions particularly well-suited to AI’s strengths in coding, mathematical manipulation, and broad knowledge access. Researcher Matthew Schwartz found that by allowing Claude to identify these problems, rather than forcing it into pre-defined research paths, significant progress could be made across diverse fields. Initial experiments with Claude Opus 4.5 demonstrated performance comparable to a graduate student, but required extensive hand-holding; BootLoops aims to overcome that impedance mismatch.

BootLoops has already facilitated novel findings in areas like ecology (revealing discrepancies in tree species change rates compared to neutral theory) and population genetics (identifying a mechanism called gene conversion). The toolkit functions as a harness, streamlining code porting and enabling exact calculations, particularly with semi-numerical bootstrap methods. The project involved 36 manuscripts with 19 co-authors across 18 fields developed in three months, with the source code available on GitHub.

The key takeaway is that focusing on problems aligned with current AI capabilities—those requiring extensive computation and drawing on interdisciplinary knowledge—can unlock valuable scientific insights. While AI won't replace the need for human scientists to guide research and interpret results, it provides a powerful new tool for accelerating discovery, especially where existing expertise is fragmented across disciplines.

Can we predict the jobs robots will do?

Anthropic has developed a “robot exposure index” to assess the potential for automation across jobs, finding that robots can currently perform 74% of physical tasks in the US, representing 34% of working hours. This assessment uses the AI model Claude to evaluate how well existing robots can perform specific job tasks within varying levels of environmental control – from purpose-built factories to unstructured outdoor environments. While robots can perform a substantial portion of physical work, they are currently cost-competitive for only 0.3% of job tasks.

The analysis indicates that jobs with higher exposure to robots tend to be held by individuals who are male, less educated, and lower paid, such as drivers and warehouse workers, while jobs like nursing and general repair remain largely protected due to the current limitations of robotic capabilities. Combining robot and LLM capabilities, 81% of work is potentially exposed to automation, though significant barriers remain including cost, capability (particularly in manipulation and dexterity), human preferences, and regulations. Historical data suggests that jobs more exposed to robots have experienced wage and employment declines over the past 50 years, but widespread adoption will likely take decades, even with continued price declines.

Zhipu AI’s GLM-5.3 demonstrates capabilities comparable to Anthropic’s Claude Mythos Preview in autonomously developing end-to-end cyber exploits. Project Swap: What happens when agents trade for us?

Anthropic’s Project Swap investigated how well AI agents – specifically those powered by Claude – can trade on behalf of people. The experiment involved 201 Anthropic employees trading books, with each employee first chatting with Claude to determine their reading preferences. Agents then negotiated with each other on a digital trading floor. The study found that agents were surprisingly good at representing their participants’ preferences based on a short (averaging 216 words) conversation, matching their rankings 61% of the time. However, the market’s efficiency was limited more by the agents’ imperfect understanding of participant preferences than by the negotiation process itself.

Further analysis revealed that the model powering the agent (Haiku, Sonnet, Opus, or Fable) had a greater impact on negotiating outcomes than the specific instructions given to the agent ("ruthless" vs. "prosocial"). Stronger models led to more efficient markets. Participants indicated they would trust such an agent with roughly 30% of their yearly book budget, about 75% of what they’d trust a human friend with. The researchers highlight the importance of verifying agent understanding of user preferences – proposing a "test" where agents demonstrate their understanding before acting on behalf of a user – and establishing clear rules for agent marketplaces to ensure positive outcomes for all involved.

The blog stresses that success relies on a well-defined certificate and clear agreement on acceptable risk levels, especially in regulated environments.

What a task costs on Opus 5.5 | Claude by Anthropic

Anthropic’s blog post details the cost implications of using Opus 5.5 within Claude Code. The core announcement is that Opus 5.5 is cheaper per token than Opus 5, achieving cost reductions through lower input and output token prices, as well as cheaper cache reads (60% reduction). A task’s cost is determined by four factors: the number of turns (iterations), cache read rates, output token type (most expensive), and the model selected.

The post emphasizes that a ‘turn’ – reprocessing the conversation – significantly impacts cost, and provides illustrative pricing examples using Opus 5.5’s list prices: $4/million input tokens, $20/million output tokens, and $0.20/million cache reads. Optimizing for fewer turns, utilizing a high cache hit rate (up to 96% possible), and minimizing unnecessary model ‘thinking’ (output tokens) are key to cost control. A 90% cache hit rate can reduce input costs from $11.20 to $1.62 for 2.8M tokens.

The post also details changes in Opus 5.5; prices are lower, and the model generally performs more work per token, potentially increasing task completion but requiring individual measurement. Tools like /usage can track token consumption, and /compact can summarize conversation history to reduce costs, though at the risk of information loss. A key recommendation is to measure task costs on your own codebase to fully understand the benefits of Opus 5.5.

Salesforce in Claude | Claude by Anthropic

Anthropic has released a beta plugin, “Salesforce in Claude,” designed to integrate Claude with Salesforce CRM. The plugin leverages 37 skills to assist sales representatives with tasks like account research, call preparation, pipeline review, and CRM updates. It connects via two connectors—Salesforce and Slack—allowing Claude to read and, with approval, write data within existing Salesforce permissions.

Key features include a daily personalized brief summarizing meetings, deals, and unread communications; assistance in preparing for calls by consolidating information from Salesforce, Slack, and email; and scoring opportunities against a team’s methodology to generate close plans. After meetings, Claude can draft follow-up emails, Slack updates, and opportunity updates for review. It also offers pipeline visualization and forecasting capabilities.

Currently, 7,000 Salesforce users at companies like GitLab, Siemens, and Legora are using the plugin. The Salesforce in Claude beta is available on all paid Claude plans and requires administrative setup to connect Salesforce and manage user access. Data from organizations is not used to train Anthropic’s models by default on Team and Enterprise plans.
